Abstract: Magnetar giant flares are thought to eject $\gtrsim 10^{-9}\,M_\odot$ of baryons, as inferred from the radio afterglow of the 2004 flare of SGR 1806-20. One proposed mechanism is a shock driven into the neutron-star crust by the hot, high-pressure layer that the flare creates above the surface. The unbound crust could undergo $r$-process nucleosynthesis and power a short-lived "nova brevis". We study this crust ejection with general-relativistic neutrino-radiation hydrodynamics simulations performed with AthenaK, using a tabulated nuclear equation of state and two-moment neutrino transport, for flare pressures corresponding to magnetic fields $B = 10^{15}$-$10^{16.5}$ G, and compute the nucleosynthesis of the ejecta with a nuclear reaction network. We find that such flares unbind $10^{-10}$ to $5\times10^{-8}\,M_\odot$ of crust. The ejecta keep the electron fraction of the crust to within a few hundredths, and weak interactions affect it mainly in the most energetic flares. The fast, high-entropy ejecta freeze out mostly as $α$ particles and free neutrons. In the stronger flares nuclei up to the third $r$-process peak do form, but those with $A \geq 90$ make up at most $\approx 8\times10^{-4}$ of the ejected mass, or $\approx 3.5\times10^{-11}\,M_\odot$ per flare, and less than $10^{-5}$ for $B \leq 10^{15.5}$ G. A nova brevis from these ejecta would be powered mainly by the decay of free neutrons. Like earlier work, our models assume spherical symmetry, a flare that covers the whole star, and no magnetic field. A field tangential to the surface would hold the crust down and weaken the shock, and a flare confined to part of the surface should eject proportionally less mass. We therefore regard our ejecta masses as upper limits for a given flare pressure, and expect realistic magnetar flares to produce negligible amounts of $r$-process material.
